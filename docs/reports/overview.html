Trade Winds & Headwinds

Twenty-Five Years of Tourism and Economic Change in the U.S. Virgin Islands, 2000–2025

Author

Bryan Johns

Published

November 2026

The central question

The U.S. Virgin Islands’ tourism economy returned through two catastrophic shocks: Hurricanes Irma and Maria in September 2017, and the COVID-19 shutdown of 2020. The first destroyed infrastructure as well as demand. The second interrupted travel again before the islands had recovered.

The useful question is not simply whether tourism is back. It is whether the economy that has reappeared is the same economy that existed before the storms.

The evidence produces a mixed answer. In 2024, published arrivals were close to the 2016 benchmark. Air arrivals exceeded it. Cruise traffic did not. Hotel rooms remained smaller, published occupancy was higher, and broad Leisure and Hospitality employment was still below its former annual average. By 2021, the latest available year for BEA accommodation and food-services value added, that industry remained below its 2016 real-dollar level as well.

Arrivals, capacity, labor, and economic output are therefore not telling the same recovery story. They show a sector that has regained a substantial flow of visitors, but has not restored its full pre-hurricane scale.

Two shocks, two kinds of disruption

The 2017 hurricanes struck in September, when much of that year’s visitation had already occurred. Their full consequences became visible in 2018. Total arrivals fell to about 86% of their 2016 level in 2018 and reached their low point in 2020.

The pandemic altered the mix. In 2020, cruise passengers fell to 25% of their 2019 level, while air visitors fell to 32%. Both have since returned, but at different speeds. By 2024, air visitors reached 109% of the 2016 level, while cruise passengers reached 90%. The published total, which includes the two published modes, stood at 97%.

These are arrivals, not visitor-days. They do not establish length of stay, number of repeat trips, or total spending.

A smaller economy serving a large flow of visitors

The visitor base is the clearest partial recovery. Capacity is not.

USVIBER’s reported hotel-room inventory stood at 3,127 rooms in 2024, 82% of the 2016 level. The Census series points to an even sharper change in one payroll-filing population: 2,039 accommodation guest rooms in 2022, down from 5,027 in 2012. Because the Census counts a different population and changed its guestroom product crosswalk in 2017, these numbers should not be combined; together, however, they describe a capacity constraint.

The result is a high published occupancy rate on a smaller inventory. USVIBER reports 66.3% occupancy in 2024, up 9.6 percentage points from 56.7% in 2016. The ratio does not mean the accommodation sector produced 66.3% of prior room nights. It means the remaining inventory was used more intensively.

Island-level hotel data, available from USVIBER’s published PDF reports, reveal where the capacity loss is concentrated. By 2024, available room-nights on St. Thomas–St. John were 75% of their 2016 level, while available room-nights on St. Croix were 95%. The territory-wide accommodation shortfall is overwhelmingly a St. Thomas–St. John story. St. Croix’s hotel capacity, measured in room-nights available, had largely returned to its 2016 level by 2024.

FRED’s Leisure and Hospitality series broadens the labor view beyond tourism alone. It includes arts, entertainment, and accommodation and food services. Its annual average reached 91% of the 2016 level in 2024 and fell to 84% in 2025. That is a broad labor signal, not a count of tourism jobs.

Spending and economic output add context, not a definitive accounting

This chart deliberately does not resolve spending and industry output onto a common unit. Nominal expenditure in millions of dollars, approximate real expenditure, and an indexed BEA series answer different questions. It is useful only as a reminder that the available economic measures did not move as neatly as arrivals.

Nominal visitor expenditure reached $1.829 billion in 2024, close to the $1.873 billion recorded in 2016. After converting it with annual mainland CPI-U, however, it was about $1.399 billion in approximate 2016 dollars. The adjustment is not a USVI cost-of-living estimate.

BEA real Accommodation and Food Services value added reached 87% of its 2016 level in 2021, the latest industry observation. This industry is broader than tourism: it includes resident-serving activity, while visitor-related activity in transportation, retail, and recreation appears elsewhere. The series cannot establish tourism GDP or isolate the effect of disaster spending. But it does fit the broader pattern of industry recovery lagging visitor arrivals.

What did not recover in the same way

Virgin Islands National Park offers a separate signal. Estimated recreation visits reached 103% of their 2016 level in 2024 and 115% in 2025. Those visits are not unique people and cannot be compared directly with territory-wide arrivals. The series also has a major counting-method change before 2022 and a separate 2026 traffic-counter approach that is excluded here.

The park’s stronger recovery may reflect a shift toward recreation, changes in visitor behavior, differences in how visits are estimated, or simply the different population the park data measure. It does not prove stronger total tourism.

What is clear is the contrast. At the same post-disruption horizon:

  • air arrivals are above the 2016 level;
  • cruise passengers remain below;
  • hotel inventory remains smaller;
  • broad Leisure and Hospitality employment remains below;
  • real Accommodation and Food Services value added remains below;
  • NPS park recreation visits are above.

The strongest interpretation

The USVI appears to have recovered much of its visitor flow, but not necessarily the full scale or structure of the tourism economy that existed in 2016. The evidence is consistent with a smaller accommodation stock operating with higher occupancy, uneven labor recovery, and visitor activity that does not map cleanly onto park or industry output.

That conclusion is not a claim that the economy has been permanently transformed. Later observations, especially for 2025 and beyond, may revise the picture. It is a statement about what can be established now: the two major shocks did not leave all indicators at the same stage of recovery.
